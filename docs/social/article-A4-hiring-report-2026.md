# The UK Private Healthcare Hiring Report 2026

*The Practice Standard's annual read on who's hiring, who's leaving, and what it costs. Flagship data asset — republish each year. ~1,300 words.*

---

## The one-line version

Private healthcare in the UK is growing faster than the workforce that staffs it — and the practices that win in 2026 won't be the ones paying the most. They'll be the ones hiring the fastest, verifying the hardest, and treating recruitment as a fixed cost instead of an emergency.

Here's the year in numbers.

---

## 1. The demand is real, and it's accelerating

The UK private healthcare market is on track to reach **£18.6bn by 2033, up from roughly £13.75bn today**. That's not a blip — it's a structural shift driven by an NHS waiting list still hovering around **7.1 million** and a public increasingly willing to pay to be seen this month instead of next year.

Self-pay is the engine. People who never considered private care are booking private GP appointments, private physiotherapy, private diagnostics — not out of ideology, but out of time. Every week somebody spends on a waiting list is a week a private practice could have helped.

**What it means for hiring:** demand for clinicians in private practice is rising across almost every discipline. The bottleneck on growth is no longer patients. It's people.

---

## 2. The workforce can't keep up — and the gaps are discipline-specific

The shortage isn't uniform. It bites hardest in four areas:

- **Veterinary.** EU vet registrations in the UK are down roughly **68% since Brexit**, while pet ownership and practice demand never fell. The result: some of the most brutal hiring conditions in healthcare, full stop.
- **Optometry.** A projected **~2,000-practitioner shortfall by 2030**, with a vacancy rate already around **10.3%**. Chairs sit empty for want of optometrists, not patients.
- **Physiotherapy.** The MSK waiting list sits at around **342,000 people**, and UK physio density (~1:1,136) lags well behind comparable countries like Australia (~1:742). Private physio demand has never been higher; the supply of clinicians is the limiting factor.
- **Aesthetics.** As regulation tightens, the gap between *available* practitioners and *appropriately registered* practitioners widens. Around **65%** of practitioners are nurses or doctors — meaning a meaningful share may not hold the registration clinics increasingly need to verify.

**What it means for hiring:** a generic "we're hiring" post won't cut it in a shortage market. The practices filling roles are reaching verified, discipline-specific candidates directly — before the competition does.

---

## 3. The hiring process itself is leaking time and money

Two numbers define the problem most practices don't measure:

- **The average UK cost-per-hire is around £6,125** — and for clinical roles, once you fold in agency fees, manager hours, advertising and locum cover for the gap, it runs higher.
- **Roughly 68% of applicants from general job boards fail initial screening.** That's not a candidate-quality story. It's a filtering failure: when registration isn't checked up front, "40 CVs" is mostly noise.

Then there's the agency tax. Standard placement fees run **15–25% of first-year salary** — up to **£11,250 on a single £45k hire**, paid once, for one introduction. Multiply that across a year of growth and recruitment quietly becomes one of the largest uncontrolled costs a practice carries.

**What it means for hiring:** the expensive part of hiring isn't the advert. It's the wasted time on unqualified applicants and the percentage-of-salary fees. Both are fixable.

---

## 4. Why clinicians are moving — and it isn't only the money

When we look at why clinicians leave the NHS or change private roles, the same five pulls come up again and again:

1. **Shorter lists** — time to actually treat the patient in front of them.
2. **More autonomy** — clinical decisions made by clinicians.
3. **Better kit** — modern equipment that works.
4. **Clearer pay** — a number, not a vibe.
5. **Less admin** — protected clinical time.

Pay matters, but it's rarely the deciding factor on its own. The practices retaining staff are the ones protecting the diary, funding CPD (a healthy budget is **£500–2,000/year**), covering indemnity, and providing real supervision. Those are the things that don't fit on a recruitment poster — and they're exactly what wins the hire.

**What it means for hiring:** you don't have to out-pay the big group down the road. You have to out-process and out-respect them.

---

## 5. The verification gap nobody talks about

Most practices still hire on trust: a CV, a registration number nobody checks, a gut feel. In a tight market that's a risk that's quietly growing — both for patient safety and for the practice's own liability.

Verifying a candidate's registration against the actual regulator — NMC, GMC, GDC, RCVS, GOC, HCPC — before they reach the shortlist does three things at once: it removes the most expensive failure mode (the hire who should never have got through the door), it cuts the screening time dramatically, and it reduces bias by anchoring the decision on competence and registration rather than a name on a page.

**What it means for hiring:** "verified" shouldn't be a premium feature. In 2026 it should be the baseline.

---

## What the practices that hired well had in common

Across everyone who filled roles cleanly this year, the pattern was consistent. They:

- **Treated hiring as a fixed cost**, not an emergency — budgeted, planned around the Q1 and September windows, and posted roles *before* the gap opened.
- **Led their job ads with the number** and named the CPD budget. Ads with a salary band attract 2–3× the qualified applicants.
- **Verified registration up front**, so the shortlist was six worth interviewing, not forty to wade through.
- **Moved fast** — replied within 24 hours and made candidates feel chosen, not processed.
- **Competed on the diary, not just the salary.**

None of that requires a bigger budget than the practice down the road. It requires a better process.

---

## The outlook for 2027

The demand curve isn't bending back. Waiting lists, an ageing population, rising chronic disease (diabetes alone is climbing faster than the workforce treating it), and a public now comfortable paying for speed all point the same way: **more private healthcare, and more pressure on the people who staff it.**

The practices that thrive won't be the ones with the deepest pockets. They'll be the ones who made hiring boring — predictable, verified, fast, fairly priced — while everyone else treated it as a crisis.
